01 · CHEAPEST TEST ON EARTH

The 1966 tree

On 30 December 1966 a physics professor driving past Haynesville, Louisiana, saw a pulsing object in the forest so bright it overwhelmed his headlights. The witnesses went back the next day and took two bark samples from the same tree — one completely black, one normal — the same afternoon. They still exist, in a private garage, unanalysed for 59 years, because every method available at the time would have had to cut into the bark.

Comparative gamma spectrometry on that matched pair is a two-day, five-figure experiment. Cesium-137 does not occur naturally in Louisiana timber, and 1966 was the tail of atmospheric weapons testing — so fallout is almost certainly the mundane answer. But the design is a matched pair from a single trunk, so if the blackened side is enriched relative to the clean side, something is very wrong. The case sits inside the Condon Report, filed as unexplained, and is now the subject of a peer-reviewed, open-access paper in Progress in Aerospace Sciences.

03 · NEVER RUN ON ANY CASE

The audio test

Thousands of people — US and Canadian officials, and a much larger "targeted individuals" population — report a localised sound with no external source. A multi-station audio capture with blind listening is the obvious test, and it is exactly the method that was used on the Stargate programme decades ago. It is cheap. It is decisive. It appears never to have been run on any such case.

The physics explains why the question is contested: the microwave auditory effect requires standoff under about half a wavelength, i.e. centimetres, which excludes it at 150–300 m on geometry alone. A pulsed infrared-laser optoacoustic device generates the sound at the target, so it has no propagation loss and near-unconstrained standoff — which is why former Havana-syndrome researchers reported no specific technical objections to that mechanism in March 2026.

05 Why this survives sixty years of free documents

The strongest argument for the cover-up requires the public to be unable to read the material. It can read it. Everything decisive has been public since the 1960s. The belief is maintained socially, and no document touches it.
  • The number collapsed when a committee decided to stop asking. The unexplained rate fell from over one in five to a few percent immediately after the 1953 Robertson Panel. That is a decision, not a discovery.
  • Everything readable was boring. 37 of 38 "Defense Intelligence Reference Documents" were FOIA-released, including the antigravity and wormhole papers — not one withheld, because there was nothing to protect. Everything mundane got redacted, denied, declassified-then-reconcealed, or had its authority lie about it.
  • One agency denied its own programme four times. "Moon-Dust Blue Fly": discontinued in 1978, records destroyed in 1986, denied to have existed in 1992, conceded in 1993.
  • Five adjudication bodies in forty-one years, four countries, one cause. 1966: staff the committee with "non-believers" to build "an impressive body of thick evidence that there is no reality to the observations." 1968: a committee selected for having no relevant expertise, at a university that took the money because "it was difficult to say no to the Air Force." 2000: a secret central mechanism, never peer reviewed, authored by someone whose name is protected by statute. 2025: peer review is inadequate without "a mystic's knack for piercing the Veil of Maya." Every one: the adjudicator was chosen, staffed, or anonymised for a prior.
  • The belief in a secret programme is a documented emergent property of ordinary bureaucracy. Congressional hearing, 9 October 1984: a man holding clearances on all 54 nuclear-contingency projects convened them and "the first ten minutes were amazing because half of them did not know the other half… and the guy would say, well, I'm with AT&T, sir." Yankee Blue is the deliberate version. 1984 is the accidental one. Both produce the same belief, and neither needs an alien.
  • Coverage tracks narrative interest, not evidentiary weight. The archivist who catalogued Spain's 1,900 declassified pages has no Wikipedia article in any language. The whistleblower whose every checkable claim has failed has a 79,495-byte English article and a longer Spanish one — in a country with no connection to his claims. A state-acknowledged adversary drone shootdown over Tehran has no article at all, because it is a defence problem, and defence problems do not get articles.
  • Seti has run the negative control and the UAP field has not read it. 250 million channels simultaneously; 100,000 galaxies examined for megastructures, nothing; the Earth–Moon and Sun–Earth libration points photographed twice to 19th magnitude for alien probes, nothing — the "black knight satellite" had a published null; a fusion-bythalfline wavelength searched across 108 objects, nothing; and hundreds of millions of candidate signals all resolved to interference. Meanwhile ʻOumuamua, a hundred-metre rock, was the field's flagship for a decade.
  • One honest correction to all of the above. The beacon-model negative is conditional. The Wow! signal was 72 seconds and has never been re-detected in 50 years. If a genuine transmission is a non-repeating handshake, the whole null does not apply, and nobody has built the instrument for that model.

06 What I got wrong — published, on the record

Five corrections. Three of them killed a finding I was proud of.
  1. Overstated the governance line. I wrote that the 2026 advisory council "reports through a governance board he has no access to." That was one inferential step too far. What the record supports is narrower and worse: the council has a board above it, and when its most senior member was asked directly whom it reports to, he did not say.
  2. Falsified my own nuclear-cluster hypothesis. I claimed the unresolved cases cluster at EM and nuclear facilities. Run against an index I did not build, the rate is about 25–30% — roughly what you would predict from where the instrumented witnesses are. It is a selection effect in the reporting apparatus, not in the sky.
  3. Mis-stated a physics figure and repeated it. I relayed "500 MW to 1,400 MW" for the 1966 case. The paper says 500–900, and its own abstract says the input is "witness-described apparent angular diameters and brightness estimates" at "a range of plausible distances." The witness's own estimate was about 1 MW. I had logged the podcast number as if it were established.
  4. Withdrew a number I asserted from memory. I wrote "AARO's own published list: typical UAPs are 1–4 metres," then repeated it in the next section without re-checking. A targeted search returned zero supporting hits. I withdrew it publicly. That is the same credibility-laundering mechanism I had been criticising all window, operating on me. The object-size finding survives on verified evidence only, at one to two orders of magnitude rather than three.
  5. Claimed a provenance analysis did not exist. It did. I said nobody had ever run network analysis on the claim set. AARO did it, in print, in March 2024, numbering its sources and cross-referencing them against participation lists. I also claimed the projection/parallax problem was unexamined; AARO lists "optical effects — such as parallax" in its own prosaic explanations. Both of my "novel" contributions were already in the report.
Two of my sources turned out to be instances of the thing I was studying: a machine-generated transcript hosted on an advocacy archive, and a blog that discloses on every page that it is AI-generated — which produced my sharpest fact-check. The primary documents I ended up relying on were free and had been available for years.

09 The decoy nobody checked for

An active, platform-integrated US capability to generate fake aircraft in the sky — and not one UAP case has ever been tested against it. Seven falsifiers, each a specific test on a file.

What the patent actually claims

US Patent 11,359,892 B2 — "System and method for laser-induced plasma for infrared homing missile countermeasure." Filed 6 Aug 2018, granted 14 Jun 2022, active, expires 2041. Assignee: United States of Navy. Navy Case 102680.

Claim 1: generate a laser-induced plasma filament, and "rastering the LIPF using optics and mirrors to generate a volumetric image in space." Claim 3: "each laser system being configured to generate a ghost image such that a plurality of air vehicles appear to be present."

The decoy moves with the aircraft, is created "less than a millionth of a second," and can be placed at any distance. David Hambling, Forbes, 2020: the objects in the released DoD video "rotate mid-air and zip along at phenomenal speed: all maneuvers which would be easy to reproduce with a phantom projected image."

Seven falsifiers — each a test on a file, not an argument

  • Occlusion failure. A projected image cannot self-occlude: parts of the shape cannot pass in front of other parts of it. Real objects show depth-ordered occlusion. Frame-by-frame test.
  • No background parallax. A projection has a fixed viewpoint. Stars or terrain must not shift relative to the decoy through a known aircraft manoeuvre.
  • Motion coupling. The patent says the decoy moves with the aircraft, not like a flare. Plot the two tracks.
  • Emission mismatch. It is plasma — optically luminous, not a clean hot-body IR signature. Compare against a flare, an engine, and a low-observable airframe.
  • Spatial coherence. Rastering implies a generated pattern. Inter-frame correlation will be phase-coherent in a way real objects are not.
  • No carrying platform. A decoy needs a launching aircraft in sensor range. If no asset is in frame, this is excluded outright — and most UAP video is naval.
  • Response to jamming. A countermeasure is built to be defeated. A real object is not.

The honest bound: this is a missile countermeasure, deployed under attack. It cannot explain Gimbal, GoFast, or Nimitz, and the inventor of the concept conceded as much. What it can do is explain swarms of orbs near a naval asset — and it means the adjudication list was never complete.
